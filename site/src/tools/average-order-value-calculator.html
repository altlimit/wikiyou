What is average order value?

Average order value (AOV) is the average amount a customer spends in a single order. It is one of the three levers that determine ecommerce revenue — traffic, conversion rate, and AOV — and it is usually the cheapest of the three to move. Buying more traffic costs money on every click. Lifting conversion rate means winning over people who have already decided not to buy. Lifting AOV means persuading someone who is already checking out to add a little more to the cart, which is why bundles, volume discounts, free-shipping thresholds, and post-purchase upsells are such common tactics.

AOV also sets the ceiling on what you can afford to pay to acquire a customer. If your AOV is $80 and your gross margin is 40%, each order contributes about $32 before overhead — so a $40 cost per acquisition loses money. Raise AOV to $100 at the same margin and that same $40 CPA becomes profitable. This is why AOV shows up in nearly every paid-acquisition model.

The AOV formula

AOV = Total Revenue ÷ Number of Orders

That is deliberately simple, but the inputs need care:

  • Same period for both numbers. Monthly revenue divided by annual orders produces a meaningless figure. Pick one window and use it for both.
  • Orders, not customers, and not units. One customer who buys three times in a month is three orders. One order containing four items is still one order.
  • Be consistent about what revenue includes. Most teams use net product revenue — after discounts and refunds, excluding shipping charges and sales tax. Including shipping and tax inflates AOV and makes it incomparable to industry figures or to your own past numbers.

The projection in this calculator applies your target uplift to AOV while holding order count constant: Projected Revenue = Total Revenue × (1 + uplift ÷ 100), and Extra Revenue = Projected Revenue − Total Revenue. Holding orders constant is the point — it isolates the value of the AOV change itself, with no assumption that you also found more customers.

How to use this calculator

Enter the total revenue for a period and the number of orders in that same period. The calculator shows your AOV immediately. Then enter a target uplift percentage — the improvement you think a bundle, a threshold, or a cross-sell could realistically produce — and you will see the new AOV, the projected revenue at the same order volume, and the extra revenue that change is worth. You can enter a negative uplift to model a decline, which is useful when weighing a discount campaign that may raise order count but drag AOV down.

Worked example

Suppose a store did $50,000 in revenue across 625 orders last month. AOV is $50,000 ÷ 625 = $80.00.

The store sets a free-shipping threshold at $95 and adds a single cross-sell on the cart page, aiming for a 10% AOV lift. If it works and order count holds steady, AOV becomes $88.00, revenue becomes $55,000, and the extra revenue is $5,000 per month — roughly $60,000 a year from the same number of customers. At a 40% gross margin that is about $2,000 a month in additional gross profit, with no extra ad spend attached to it.

The honest caveat: an AOV lift is a hypothesis until you measure it. Free-shipping thresholds can suppress orders from small-basket shoppers, and aggressive upsells can hurt conversion. Track AOV alongside conversion rate and total revenue, not on its own — a rising AOV with falling order count can leave you flat or worse. Comparing a period before and after a change, or running a proper A/B test, tells you what actually happened.

A note on benchmarks

Published AOV benchmarks vary enormously by category — a furniture store and a cosmetics store have almost nothing in common on this metric — and they also drift by season and by traffic source. Your own trailing AOV is a far more useful baseline than any industry average, so this calculator deliberately does not compare you to one.
