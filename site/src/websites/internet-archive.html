Overview

The Internet Archive is an American non-profit digital library based in San Francisco, California. It collects and preserves digital material — archived web pages, digitized books and texts, audio recordings, films and video, software, and images — and makes most of it available to the public free of charge through its website, archive.org. Its stated mission is to provide “universal access to all knowledge”.

The organization is best known for the Wayback Machine, a public interface to its archive of the World Wide Web, which lets readers view earlier versions of web pages, including pages that have since changed or disappeared.

History

The Internet Archive was founded in 1996 by Brewster Kahle. Its first work was archiving the web itself: from its founding it began collecting periodic copies of publicly accessible web pages, on the reasoning that material published online was being lost as sites changed or went offline, with no library keeping a record of it.

The web archive was opened to the public as the Wayback Machine in 2001. Over time the organization broadened its scope from the web to other forms of media, digitizing printed books and hosting collections of audio, video and software alongside material uploaded by the public.

Wayback Machine

The Wayback Machine lets a reader enter the address of a web page and browse the copies of it that the Archive has captured, arranged by date. Captures are made by automated web crawlers; the Archive also offers a Save Page Now feature with which anyone can request that a specific page be captured immediately.

The archive is not a complete record of the web. Pages are captured at irregular intervals, some are never captured at all, and content generated dynamically or placed behind a login is often missing or only partly preserved. Site owners can also ask for their pages to be excluded. Within those limits, the Wayback Machine is widely used to cite and check web pages as they appeared at a given time.

For libraries, archives and other institutions that want to build their own web collections, the Internet Archive runs Archive-It, a subscription web-archiving service. It also developed Heritrix, an open-source web crawler used for archival crawling.

Collections

Beyond the web archive, archive.org hosts a wide range of collections, including:

  • Texts — Books and other printed material digitized by the Archive, many of them in the public domain and free to read and download.
  • Audio — Recordings of many kinds, including the Live Music Archive of concert recordings by artists who permit their performances to be taped and shared.
  • Video — Films, including public-domain films, and other moving-image material.
  • Software — Historical software and video games, many of which can be run directly in a web browser through emulation.

Members of the public can create accounts and upload their own material to the site, so the collections combine items curated by the Archive with contributions from users.

Book Lending

The Internet Archive runs Open Library, a project that aims to create a web page for every book ever published. Through it, the Archive lends digitized copies of print books under a model it calls controlled digital lending, in which each scanned book can be borrowed by as many readers at a time as the number of physical copies the lending library holds.

In 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the Archive opened a temporary National Emergency Library that removed those borrowing limits. Several major publishers — Hachette, HarperCollins, John Wiley & Sons and Penguin Random House — sued over its lending of copyrighted books, in the case known as Hachette v. Internet Archive. A United States federal court ruled in the publishers’ favour in 2023, and the decision was upheld on appeal in 2024.
